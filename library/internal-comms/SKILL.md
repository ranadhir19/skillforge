# internal-comms

## Purpose

Write internal communications people actually read: the decision or ask
up front, impact in plain language, and the next update promised at a
stated time.

## Procedure

1. **Summary first.** Two sentences: what happened (or is asked), and
   what it means for the reader. Everything else is supporting detail
   most readers will skim — write so the skimmers still get it right.
2. **Impact in the reader's terms.** Not "checkout success degraded" —
   "about 4 in 10 checkouts failed for 47 minutes". Numbers the reader
   can feel, sourced from the incident record.
3. **Current status, honestly tense-marked.** What is fixed, what is
   still open, what is being watched. Never use the past tense for a
   problem that is still present.
4. **Next update at a stated time.** Even if the update is "no change".
   Silence during an incident reads as chaos; a promised cadence reads
   as control.
5. **One ask, clearly owned.** If readers must do something, say what,
   who, and by when — once, in its own line.

## Rules

- Write for stakeholders, not for the engineers who fixed it (that
  document is the handoff/postmortem — a different skill).
- No blame, no heroics; mechanisms and timelines only.
